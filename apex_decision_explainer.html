The Core Idea
What This Model Does
APEX is not a stock picker. It never forms a view on any individual company. Instead, it learns which combinations of price-based signals across all 110 Nasdaq-100 names simultaneously have historically been associated with better risk-adjusted returns relative to QQQ. The question it's answering every week is not "will Apple go up?" — it's "given what the market looks like right now, what allocation across 110 names has beaten QQQ on a Sharpe basis?"
How It Learns
Two components work together: the Actor (makes the allocation call) and the Critic (evaluates whether it was good). Over thousands of weekly decisions across 8 rolling 6-year training windows, the Actor's judgment is continuously refined by the Critic's feedback — getting progressively better at recognizing which signal combinations call for which allocation response.
The Goal
Outperform QQQ on a Sharpe basis. Not just beat the index in raw return, but do it with less downside risk, lower drawdowns, and without excessive turnover. The benchmark is always the reference point — making money in a down market matters more than making slightly less money in an up one.

Stage 1  ·  The Market Snapshot
Stage 1 — Observe
The Market Snapshot
Every Friday close, the model takes a complete picture of the entire Nasdaq-100 universe before making any decision. Think of it as reading the whole market simultaneously — not just where prices are today, but how every name has behaved over the past 60 trading days, how each stock compares to every other stock right now, and what the broader macro environment looks like.
What the Model Is Actually Looking At

For each of the 110 names in the universe, the model tracks 25 signals per stock going back 60 trading days. These signals fall into two categories that are worth distinguishing.

Time-Series Signals (17 per stock)

How this stock has behaved over time — its own momentum at different horizons (1 week, 4 weeks, 13 weeks), how volatile it's been at different window lengths, where it sits relative to its own Bollinger Bands, its RSI, and its beta to the market. These are absolute measures of the stock's recent history.

Cross-Sectional Signals (8 per stock)

How this stock compares to every other stock in the universe right now. A stock in the 90th percentile of momentum doesn't just mean it went up — it means it outperformed 90% of the other Nasdaq-100 names. These relative rankings are what create the opportunity to add alpha in the first place.

The Bigger Picture: Global Context

On top of the 25 per-stock signals, the model reads 20 global context variables that tell it about the environment the portfolio is operating in — things like where VIX is, whether the macro backdrop is risk-on or risk-off, what QQQ itself has been doing, and the current state of the portfolio (how concentrated it is, what last week's turnover was).

This global context matters because the same momentum signal means something different in a low-volatility trending market versus a high-volatility risk-off environment. The model needs both the stock-level picture and the macro picture to interpret signals correctly.

Concrete Example
Say it's late 2023 and the Magnificent 7 are running hard. The snapshot shows that cross-sectional momentum is extremely concentrated — the top 7 names are pulling away from the rest of the universe with vol compressing. It also shows that the 110-name universe has unusually low return dispersion — almost everything is moving together. These are concrete, measurable signals. No interpretation required yet. That's the next step's job.
The Key Point
The model is not reading news. It has no knowledge of earnings, analyst upgrades, or management commentary. Everything it sees is encoded in price — how stocks have moved, how they've moved relative to each other, and what the macro environment looks like in price-based terms. The richness of the snapshot is in its breadth: 110 names, 60 days, 25 signals each, all at once.
Stage 2  ·  Finding the Pattern
Stage 2 — Encode
Finding the Pattern
The neural encoder takes the full market snapshot — 110 stocks, 60 days, 25 signals per name — and compresses it into a single fingerprint of what this market environment looks like. This fingerprint is the accumulated product of training across thousands of weekly snapshots and multiple market cycles. The encoder has learned, implicitly, which combinations of signals carry predictive information and which are noise.
What "Encoding" Actually Means

The raw snapshot contains an enormous amount of data. Before any portfolio decision can be made, the model needs to distill that data into something it can act on — a compact representation that captures the structure of the current market environment.

Think of it this way: a veteran portfolio manager doesn't consciously process 60 days of price data for 110 stocks before making a decision. Over years of experience they develop an intuition for market regimes — they recognize certain patterns instinctively and know how to respond. The neural encoder is doing something analogous. It has processed thousands of weekly snapshots and learned to extract the features that consistently carry useful information across different market environments.

Example — Regime Recognition Without Labels
Say it's mid-2022 and volatility is spiking. Short-term momentum is negative across most names, VIX is elevated, and cross-sectional dispersion has widened. The encoder has seen this fingerprint before — across multiple training windows covering different volatility regimes. It doesn't label this as "a risk-off environment." It just recognizes the pattern and produces a fingerprint that will lead the Actor toward a more defensive allocation. The label emerges implicitly from the decision.
Trained Across 8 Market Cycles

The encoder's pattern recognition is trained across 8 rolling 6-year windows — each sliding 2 years forward through history, from 2005 all the way to the present. This means the encoder has seen multiple bull markets, a financial crisis, a pandemic crash, rate shocks, and everything in between. The patterns it recognizes are not calibrated to a single market era.

Critical Distinction
The encoder does not output a prediction ("momentum will continue") or a label ("this is a risk-off regime"). It outputs a vector of numbers — a fingerprint — that the Actor has learned to map to portfolio weights. The "intelligence" lives entirely in the mapping between fingerprints and allocations, built up over thousands of training iterations.
Stage 3  ·  The Portfolio Manager (Actor)
Stage 3 — Decide
The Portfolio Manager
The Actor is the part of the model that makes the actual investment decision. Given the market fingerprint, it produces a score for every stock in the universe. Higher score means more exposure wanted. Those scores get converted into portfolio weights. The Actor has no memory, no opinion on any company, and no ability to evaluate whether its decisions are good — that's the Critic's job. It just applies the mapping it has learned: market looks like this, allocation should look like that.
How the Actor Makes a Decision

The Actor passes the market fingerprint through its neural network and outputs a raw score for each of the 110 names. Think of it as a relative attractiveness ranking — a higher score means the model's learned mapping says "more exposure is warranted here given what the market looks like right now."

Those raw scores get converted into portfolio weights through a mathematical transformation that ensures they sum to 1 and are all positive. The result is the Actor's proposed allocation — what it wants to hold before the hard rules are applied in the next step.

The Most Important Distinction
The Actor is not predicting which stocks will go up or down. It is not forming a view on any company. It is recognizing a market pattern and applying a learned mapping from that pattern to an allocation that has historically produced better risk-adjusted outcomes relative to QQQ. The entire "view" is encoded in the signal fingerprint — there is no fundamental judgment happening here.
What This Looks Like in Practice
Example — Low-Vol Grind
Say it's mid-2019. The market is in a slow, low-volatility uptrend with tight cross-sectional dispersion — almost everyone in QQQ is going up together. The Actor has learned that in this environment, chasing the recent leaders is a crowded and unrewarding trade. It nudges the allocation toward something closer to equal-weight and lets the index do the work. A seasoned PM would recognize this as avoiding momentum crowding at low-vol peaks. The Actor didn't learn that concept — it just learned the signal fingerprint.
Example — Post-Selloff Setup
Say it's after a sharp 3-week selloff. Short-term momentum is deeply negative but the 13-week return is still positive for a subset of names, and vol is starting to compress off the peak. The Actor has seen this fingerprint before: overweighting the cohort where medium-term momentum is intact and vol is normalizing has historically improved Sharpe over the following 4 weeks. It doesn't know a reversion is coming — it just recognizes the pattern.
Example — Defensive Tech Rotation
Say it's mid-2022, VIX is elevated, and short-term momentum is negative across most names. The Actor has learned that when this signal combination appears, overweighting defensive tech — cash-heavy, low-beta names — and trimming high-multiple growth has historically held up better relative to QQQ. Not a prediction. A learned allocation response.
The Key Point
The Actor is a learned function, not a forecaster. Given the market fingerprint, it outputs an allocation. The quality of that allocation depends entirely on how well the training process has calibrated the Actor's mapping — which is what the Critic (Stage 6) is responsible for improving over time.
Stage 4  ·  The Hard Rules
Stage 4 — Constrain
The Hard Rules
Before any trade is executed, the proposed allocation goes through a constraint layer that enforces non-negotiable mandate rules. This step is not machine learning — it is deterministic math that runs every single time, without exception. No signal, no matter how strong, can override these limits.
The Four Rules
Long Only
No short positions, ever. The model operates within a long-only mandate identical to a standard active equity fund.
Fully Invested
Weights always sum to 100%. The model cannot go to cash — it is always expressing a view on relative allocation within the Nasdaq-100 universe.
20% Per-Name Cap
No single stock can exceed 20% of the portfolio. If the Actor wants 35% in Apple, it gets capped at 20% — the excess is redistributed proportionally across the remaining names.
50% Sector Cap
No single GICS sector can exceed 50% of the portfolio. Prevents the model from making catastrophic concentrated sector bets even when the signal is very strong in one area.
Why This Matters for Risk Management

The constraint layer is the risk manager sitting between the portfolio manager and the trading desk. The Actor can become confident in a signal — perhaps overconfidently so — but the hard rules cap the damage. A model that learns to overweight semiconductors at 70% of the portfolio whenever momentum is strong would have catastrophic drawdowns when the signal reverses. The 50% sector cap prevents that regime from ever being tested in production.

Importantly, the constraints do not override the model's signal — they trim the edges of it. The Actor still controls the direction of every allocation decision. The constraints just ensure no single bet is large enough to be catastrophic.

Example — How It Works in Practice
Say the Actor's signal is very strong in mega-cap tech. It wants to hold Apple at 28%, Microsoft at 22%, Nvidia at 18%, and Google at 15% — a total tech concentration of roughly 65%. The constraint layer caps Apple at 20%, Microsoft at 20%, and ensures the tech sector overall doesn't exceed 50%. The "trimmed" weight gets redistributed proportionally across the rest of the book. The Actor's preference for tech is still expressed — it's just bounded.
The Key Point
The constraint layer is the one part of the pipeline that never adapts. The Actor, the Critic, and the neural encoder all get updated through training. The rules stay constant. They are the mandate — and the model is trained knowing they exist, so over time it learns to propose allocations that don't need heavy trimming.
Stage 5  ·  Execution & Grade
Stage 5 — Execute
Execution & Grade
The constrained portfolio weights become actual trades, executed at next-morning open prices. The week plays out. At close on Friday, the model's performance is scored using a reward function that defines what "good" means — not just did we make money, but did we beat QQQ on a risk-adjusted basis, and did we do it without excessive turnover or concentrated downside exposure.
How Performance Gets Scored

The reward function has five components. Understanding each one explains the model's behavior — because the model will, over time, learn to optimize exactly for what this function rewards and penalizes.

Benchmark-Relative Return
How much we outperformed or underperformed QQQ this week, scaled by how volatile the market was. Outperforming in a high-vol week is worth more than the same outperformance in a calm week. This is the primary alpha signal — always measured relative to the benchmark, never in absolute terms.
Downside Risk Penalty
A penalty proportional to the portfolio's own realized downside volatility. The model is punished for taking on excess risk beyond what the market itself is exhibiting. Encourages the model to hold lower-vol names when the signal is ambiguous rather than concentration risk.
Asymmetric Loss Penalty
Drawdowns are penalized harder than equivalent gains are rewarded. A week where we significantly trail QQQ costs more in the reward function than an equivalent outperformance earns. Trains the model to prioritize capital preservation — a key differentiator from raw return maximization.
Transaction Cost Penalty
A direct penalty for every dollar of turnover — commission, bid-ask spread, and market impact. The model learns that high turnover is a drag it needs to overcome with signal quality. Prevents the model from over-trading small, noisy signals.
Constraint Violation Penalty
A penalty for any week where the portfolio breaches its mandate constraints. In practice, the constraint projector prevents violations — this acts as a reinforcing backstop.
Why the Benchmark Framing Changes Everything
In a week where QQQ drops 3%, if the portfolio is down only 1%, that scores very well — not because we made money, but because we significantly outperformed on the downside. In a week where QQQ is up 2% and the portfolio is up 1.5%, that scores poorly despite a positive absolute return. The model is never asked "did you make money?" It is asked "did you add value relative to the benchmark?"
What Happens to This Information

After scoring, the complete record of what the model observed, what it decided, and how that decision performed gets stored in a replay buffer — essentially a memory of recent weeks. This is what the Critic draws on in Stage 6 to evaluate and improve the Actor's decision-making. The replay buffer holds recent transactions with an emphasis on more recent ones, reflecting that market dynamics shift over time.

The Key Point
The reward function is the most consequential design decision in the entire system. It defines what the model is being trained to optimize. Get it wrong and you get a model that technically maximizes the reward while doing something you didn't intend — like concentrating risk or avoiding trades that would add real alpha. The five-term structure is designed to align the model's incentives with what a real active equity manager would actually want.
Stage 6  ·  The Analyst (Critic)
Stage 6 — Learn
The Analyst
The Critic is the feedback mechanism that makes the whole system learn over time. It doesn't make portfolio decisions — it evaluates them. Every week, it asks: given the market state we observed and the allocation the Actor chose, what cumulative risk-adjusted return should we have expected? Then it compares that prediction against what actually happened, sharpens its own judgment, and sends a refined signal back to the Actor telling it how to improve. This loop, repeated across thousands of weekly decisions, is how the model gets better.
The Actor and Critic: Two Different Jobs
The Actor (Portfolio Manager)

Makes allocation decisions. Takes the market fingerprint, outputs portfolio weights. Has no mechanism to evaluate whether its decisions are good — it simply applies its learned mapping. Does not predict outcomes.

The Critic (Risk Analyst)

Evaluates allocation decisions. Given the same market state and the Actor's chosen weights, predicts the cumulative risk-adjusted return. Compares predictions to reality. Never touches portfolio weights directly — only sends feedback to the Actor.

How the Learning Loop Actually Works

At time T, the Critic looks at the market state and the weights the Actor chose, and makes a prediction: "given this state and this allocation, I expect the cumulative discounted reward from here to be X." That is the Critic's forecast — not the Actor's.

The week plays out. The reward arrives. From the new market state at T+1, the Critic produces a fresh estimate of value going forward. Combining the actual reward earned with that new forward-looking estimate gives you a target — what the Critic should have predicted at time T, in hindsight.

The difference between the Critic's original prediction and that target is the error. The Critic updates itself to shrink that error, becoming a more accurate evaluator. Then it tells the Actor: here is how sensitive my score is to small changes in your output — move your weights in the direction that would have produced an allocation I score more highly. The Actor takes a small step in that direction.

Do that across thousands of weekly decisions over a 6-year training window, and the Actor's learned mapping gradually converges toward allocations the Critic considers genuinely predictive of risk-adjusted outperformance — not lucky outcomes, but patterns with consistent predictive power across different market environments.

Example — What the Feedback Signal Looks Like
The Critic doesn't say "that was a bad trade." It says: "I expected that allocation to earn X in cumulative risk-adjusted return over the next 4 steps. We actually earned Y. That gap tells me this particular signal combination — elevated short-term momentum in a high-vol environment — doesn't predict outperformance as reliably as I estimated. Here is the gradient: the Actor should weight this pattern less heavily next time." It's attribution analysis, running automatically, every week, across the entire history.
The Twin Critic Structure

There are actually two Critics running simultaneously, each learning independently from the same history. The Actor is always trained against whichever Critic is more pessimistic about a given allocation.

The intuition is straightforward from a risk management perspective: if you have two independent analysts who disagree on whether a trade is good, you defer to the more conservative one. This prevents the Actor from learning to exploit blind spots in a single Critic's model — the investment management equivalent of gaming a performance metric.

What Happens After Training

By the time the model is deployed, the Critic is retired. Only the Actor runs at inference — the portfolio manager. The Critic was the teacher; the Actor is what you actually run in production. But the Critic's judgment has been permanently baked into the Actor's weights through training. Every allocation decision the deployed Actor makes reflects the accumulated feedback of thousands of Critic evaluations across multiple market cycles.

The Key Point
The reason the Actor-Critic structure works is the same reason you don't let a portfolio manager grade their own trades. The Actor has no way of knowing whether its allocations are good — it just produces them. The Critic provides the external evaluation signal. Over time, the Actor's learned mapping converges toward the Critic's definition of a good allocation — which is calibrated to risk-adjusted outperformance of QQQ across multiple market cycles.
